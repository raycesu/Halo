# Project Accessibility Report

## Principles of Universal Design

### 1. Equitable Use

Halo provides the same core observation and constellation features to all users through one interface. However, because the sky map is primarily visual, users with low vision or blindness may have difficulty using it. Future versions could provide screen-reader labels and a text-based list describing visible celestial bodies.

### 2. Flexibility in Use

Users can choose their observation location, date, and time and can either explore the sky or create custom constellations. Users can also choose different colours for their custom constellation lines. Keyboard navigation and alternative input methods could make the program suitable for a wider range of users.

### 3. Simple and Intuitive Use

The interface uses clearly labelled fields and buttons such as "Change Observation," "Save Constellation," and "Exit." The workflow follows a clear sequence: enter the observation information, view the sky, select stars, and save the constellation.

### 4. Perceptible Information

Halo communicates its status using visible information, including the selected-star count, coloured constellation lines, and success or error messages. In the future, icons, higher-contrast colour options, and non-colour indicators could make this information easier to perceive.

### 5. Tolerance for Error

The Custom Constellation use case prevents invalid data from being saved. It rejects blank names, selections with fewer than two stars, duplicate stars, and constellation names that already exist. An appropriate error message explains why the constellation could not be saved.

### 6. Low Physical Effort

Most features can be completed using a small number of clicks and do not require repeated or complex actions. However, selecting small stars precisely may be difficult for some users. Larger selection areas, keyboard controls, and an undo option could reduce the required physical effort.

### 7. Size and Space for Approach and Use

The observation controls are grouped together and separated from the main sky map, helping users identify the available actions. However, small text and star-selection targets may be difficult for users with visual or motor impairments. Future versions could support adjustable text sizes, larger buttons, and a responsive layout for different screen sizes and user needs.

## Target Audience

Halo is intended primarily for beginner stargazers, astronomy students, teachers, and astronomy hobbyists who want an accessible way to explore the night sky. It could be marketed to schools, science clubs, families, and individuals interested in learning about visible stars and constellations. Users can enter an observation location, date, and time, view celestial bodies in the sky, consider weather conditions, and create their own constellations. Halo focuses on an approachable and educational experience rather than the advanced tools required for professional astronomical research.

## Demographics and Potential Barriers

Some groups may be less likely to use Halo because its sky map relies heavily on visual information and precise mouse input. Users who are blind or have low vision may have difficulty interpreting stars and constellation lines, while users with motor impairments may find small selection targets difficult to click. Older users may also experience barriers caused by small text, unfamiliar controls, or limited contrast. As discussed in the E3I User Diversity module, failing to consider diverse users can cause tangible harm by limiting access to educational and recreational opportunities, as well as relational harm by communicating that some users' needs are less important. Future versions of Halo could reduce these barriers by providing screen-reader support, text descriptions of the sky, scalable text, high-contrast options, keyboard navigation, and larger selection targets.
